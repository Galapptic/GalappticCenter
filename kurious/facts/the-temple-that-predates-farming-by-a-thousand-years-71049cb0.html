The Temple That Predates Farming by a Thousand Years

Twelve thousand years ago, hunter-gatherers who had no crops, no permanent homes, and no metal tools somehow carved and erected stone pillars weighing up to 20 tons.

In 1994, a German archaeologist named Klaus Schmidt was surveying a hilltop in southeastern Turkey when he noticed something odd. Local farmers had been plowing around what they assumed were medieval grave markers for generations. Schmidt recognized them as something far older. What he found buried beneath the surface would overturn a fundamental assumption about human civilization: that people had to settle down and grow food before they could build anything monumental.

The site was called Göbekli Tepe, Turkish for 'Potbelly Hill.' Over the next two decades, Schmidt and his team would uncover massive T-shaped limestone pillars arranged in circles, carved with intricate reliefs of animals, and erected with astonishing precision. Radiocarbon dating placed the oldest structures at around 9600 BCE—roughly 11,600 years ago. That's a thousand years before the first evidence of domesticated wheat, six thousand years before Stonehenge, and seven thousand years before the Great Pyramid of Giza.
