Ancient Romans Were Obsessed With Sauce Made From Rotten Fish

The most expensive condiment in Roman history was essentially liquified decomposing fish guts that could cost more than perfume.

Garum was ancient Rome's ketchup, Worcestershire sauce, and soy sauce all rolled into one pungent package. Romans drizzled this fermented fish sauce on virtually everything—meat, vegetables, fruit, even desserts. The production process would horrify modern health inspectors: workers layered fish intestines, blood, and small whole fish with salt in large vats, then left them to rot in the Mediterranean sun for months. The resulting amber liquid was strained off and bottled. Top-quality garum from Spain's southern coast fetched astronomical prices, sometimes rivaling expensive perfumes. Roman cookbooks rarely omitted it, and soldiers received garum rations. The smell was so overpowering that production was banned within city limits.
