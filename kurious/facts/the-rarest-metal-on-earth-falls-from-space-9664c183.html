The Rarest Metal on Earth Falls From Space

Most of Earth's rhenium sank into the planet's core four billion years ago, so the traces we mine today arrived later—inside meteorites.

Rhenium is one of the last stable elements humans ever discovered, identified in Germany in 1925. It took so long because there's almost none of it here. The entire Earth's crust contains roughly one part per billion of rhenium—making it rarer than gold, platinum, or even the famously scarce element iridium. But the story of why it's so rare leads back to the violent birth of our planet and the cosmic delivery service that restocked our supply.

When Earth was young and molten, heavy elements sank toward the core while lighter ones floated up to form the crust and mantle. Rhenium, with its extreme density and affinity for iron, should have followed the heavy metals down into the unreachable depths. By all logic, the surface of our planet should be essentially rhenium-free. Yet we have enough to build jet engines and refine gasoline. That rhenium came from somewhere else entirely.
