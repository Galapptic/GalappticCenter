The Two Men Who Touched the Sky First Kept a Secret

For decades, Hillary and Tenzing refused to say which of them actually stepped onto Everest's summit first—and their reason reveals more about mountaineering than any record ever could.

At 11:30 in the morning on May 29, 1953, two exhausted men stood where no human had ever stood before: 29,032 feet above sea level, on the highest point of Earth. Edmund Hillary, a New Zealand beekeeper, and Tenzing Norgay, a Nepali Sherpa, had just solved what mountaineers called the "Third Pole"—a puzzle that had killed at least thirteen climbers and defeated expedition after expedition for three decades. They stayed on the summit for about fifteen minutes. They took photographs. They buried some sweets and a small cross in the snow. And then they began a descent that would change both their lives forever.

When they returned to the world below, one question pursued them relentlessly: who stepped on top first? Hillary and Tenzing made a pact. They would say only that they reached the summit together, as a team. For years, neither man broke that silence.
