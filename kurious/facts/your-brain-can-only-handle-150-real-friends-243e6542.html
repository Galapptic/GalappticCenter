Your Brain Can Only Handle 150 Real Friends

A British anthropologist discovered that the size of your social circle isn't limited by time or technology—it's hardwired into the physical structure of your brain.

Robin Dunbar was studying monkeys in the 1990s when he noticed something odd. Primate species that lived in larger groups also had larger neocortexes—the wrinkly outer layer of the brain responsible for complex thought. The relationship was so consistent that Dunbar, an anthropologist at Oxford, wondered if it applied to humans too. He plugged our neocortex size into his equation and got a number: 150.

That figure, now known as Dunbar's number, represents the maximum number of stable social relationships a human brain can maintain. Not acquaintances you'd recognize at the grocery store, but people you know well enough to have a drink with if you bumped into them at an airport. People whose social position relative to you and everyone else you actually understand.
