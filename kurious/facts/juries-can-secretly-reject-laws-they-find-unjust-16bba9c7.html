Juries Can Secretly Reject Laws They Find Unjust

Twelve ordinary citizens sitting in a courtroom hold a power that no judge, prosecutor, or politician can override—the ability to simply refuse to convict.

Here's something they don't teach in civics class: juries can acquit defendants even when the evidence clearly proves guilt. It's called jury nullification, and it's been part of common law for centuries. If jurors believe a law is unjust, or that applying it in a particular case would be wrong, they can simply vote not guilty. The defendant walks free, and there's nothing anyone can do about it. Judges won't tell you this power exists. In fact, mentioning nullification during jury selection can get you dismissed immediately. Prosecutors hate it. But once those deliberation room doors close, twelve citizens become the final word on justice—answerable to no one.
