Trial by Jury Began as an Escape From Royal Justice

The jury system wasn't created to protect citizens—it was invented as a loophole for defendants desperate to avoid the king's brutal courts.

Before juries existed, English courts settled disputes through ordeals—burning iron, boiling water, or trial by combat. These were considered God's judgment, administered under royal authority. But in 1215, the Catholic Church banned clergy from participating in ordeals, leaving a judicial vacuum. Defendants facing the king's justice suddenly had no divine arbiter. The solution? Let local freemen decide guilt or innocence instead. This wasn't idealism—it was pragmatism. The Crown needed cases resolved, and gathering twelve neighbors who knew the accused seemed efficient. Defendants actually had to consent to jury trial, and many initially refused, preferring to take their chances with older methods. The jury was essentially a legal escape hatch that nobody expected to become civilization's cornerstone of justice.
