Protecting Yourself Can Make You a Criminal Depending on Where You Stand

The same act of self-defense that makes you a hero in Texas could land you in prison in the Netherlands—and the line between victim and criminal often comes down to geography, timing, and inches.

In 2012, a Montana man named Brice Harper shot and killed a German exchange student who had entered his garage. Harper had set a trap: he left the garage door open, placed a purse inside as bait, and waited in the dark with a shotgun. When 17-year-old Diren Dede crept in around midnight, Harper fired. Under Montana's Castle Doctrine, residents have broad rights to defend their homes. But Harper was convicted of deliberate homicide. The jury decided that lying in wait transformed self-defense into something else entirely.

The case captured something that surprises most people: self-defense isn't a universal right with clear boundaries. It's a patchwork of conflicting philosophies, historical accidents, and cultural assumptions about when violence becomes justified. What counts as reasonable force in one jurisdiction is murder in another. And the consequences of getting it wrong—even by inches or seconds—can mean decades in prison.
