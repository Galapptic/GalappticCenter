Western Scientists Refused to Accept Umami for Almost 100 Years

A Japanese chemist discovered a fifth basic taste in 1908, but Western science dismissed it as Asian folklore until the 1990s.

In 1908, Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda was enjoying a bowl of kelp broth when he noticed something peculiar. The savory deliciousness couldn't be explained by the four accepted tastes: sweet, sour, salty, or bitter. He isolated the compound responsible—glutamate—and named this sensation 'umami,' meaning 'pleasant savory taste.' Ikeda even commercialized his discovery as MSG seasoning. But Western scientists weren't buying it. For nearly a century, they insisted umami was simply a combination of existing tastes or an 'Asian cultural concept.' The idea that millions of people had been right about a distinct taste was apparently too much to accept.
