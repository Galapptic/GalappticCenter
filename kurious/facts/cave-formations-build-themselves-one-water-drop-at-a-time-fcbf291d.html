Cave Formations Build Themselves One Water Drop at a Time

That impressive stalactite hanging from a cave ceiling might have taken 100,000 years to grow just one inch, with each mineral-laden droplet adding an almost invisible layer.

When you walk through a cave and see those spectacular stone icicles hanging from the ceiling or rising from the floor, you're witnessing one of Earth's slowest construction projects. Stalactites grow downward from cave ceilings, while stalagmites build upward from the ground. Both form through an incredibly patient process: water seeps through limestone rock above, dissolving tiny amounts of calcium carbonate along the way. When each droplet reaches the cave and meets the air, it releases carbon dioxide and deposits a microscopic ring of mineral. Over thousands upon thousands of years, these rings accumulate into the magnificent formations we see today. Most grow only about 0.1 to 10 centimeters per thousand years.
