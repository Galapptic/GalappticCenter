This Fuzzy Mold Created Japan's Most Iconic Flavors

A single microscopic fungus is responsible for sake, soy sauce, miso, and the very concept of umami—yet most people have never heard its name.

Aspergillus oryzae, affectionately called koji, is a domesticated mold that has been transforming Japanese cuisine for over a thousand years. This fuzzy white-green fungus does something remarkable: it produces powerful enzymes that break down starches into sugars and proteins into amino acids. Without koji, there would be no sake, no soy sauce, no miso paste, no mirin. The mold is cultivated on steamed rice or barley, where it spreads its delicate filaments and secretes its enzymatic magic. Japanese brewers have been carefully nurturing koji strains for generations, treating them like precious family heirlooms. In 2006, Japan officially designated Aspergillus oryzae as the national fungus—the only country to honor a mold this way.
