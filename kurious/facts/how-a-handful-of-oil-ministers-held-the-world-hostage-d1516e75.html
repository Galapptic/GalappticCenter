How a Handful of Oil Ministers Held the World Hostage

In October 1973, a group of men in Kuwait City made a decision that would triple oil prices overnight, crash Western economies, and permanently shift the balance of global power.

The meeting lasted barely three hours. On October 16, 1973, oil ministers from six Persian Gulf nations gathered at the Sheraton Hotel in Kuwait City and voted to raise the posted price of crude oil by 70 percent—from $3.01 to $5.11 per barrel. The next day, they went further: a total embargo on oil shipments to the United States and the Netherlands for their support of Israel in the Yom Kippur War. Within months, oil prices quadrupled. Gas stations across America ran dry. The postwar economic order, built on cheap energy, began to crack.

It was the moment the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries—OPEC—announced itself as a force capable of reshaping the world. But the cartel had existed for thirteen years before it found the leverage to matter. Understanding how oil became a weapon means understanding the long, patient accumulation of power by nations that had been exploited for decades.
