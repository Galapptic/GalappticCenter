Ancient Greeks Built a Computer 2000 Years Before Electronics

Discovered in a shipwreck, a corroded lump of bronze turned out to be the world's first known analog computer, capable of predicting eclipses decades in advance.

In 1901, sponge divers exploring a Roman shipwreck near the Greek island of Antikythera hauled up a mysterious corroded mass. For decades, nobody understood what it was. Then X-ray analysis revealed an astonishing secret: inside were at least 30 precisely cut bronze gears, forming an intricate mechanical calculator. Dating to around 100 BCE, the Antikythera Mechanism could predict solar and lunar eclipses, track the positions of the five known planets, and calculate the dates of the ancient Olympic Games. Users simply turned a hand crank, and pointers on multiple dials showed celestial positions for any chosen date. Nothing this sophisticated would appear again for over a thousand years.
