The Letter J Was a Medieval Typo That Stuck Around

For most of written history, Julius Caesar spelled his name with an I, because the letter J simply didn't exist.

The letter J is surprisingly young—it didn't become a distinct letter until around the 16th century. In Latin and early English, I did double duty as both a vowel and a consonant. Medieval scribes began adding a tail to the letter I when it appeared at the end of Roman numerals, like writing 'xiij' instead of 'xiii.' This flourish helped prevent forgery and made numbers easier to read. Over time, scribes started using this elongated I (which looked like our J) at the beginning of words too. What started as decorative handwriting gradually evolved into a completely separate letter with its own sound. The alphabet literally grew by accident.
