The Fossil That Proved Humans Walked Upright Three Million Years Ago

A single knee joint, found in Ethiopian dirt on a November afternoon in 1974, would overturn everything scientists thought they knew about when our ancestors stood up.

On November 24, 1974, paleoanthropologist Donald Johanson was walking along a gully in the Afar region of Ethiopia when he spotted a fragment of arm bone poking from the sediment. Within hours, his team had unearthed pieces of a skull, a pelvis, ribs, and vertebrae. By the time the sun set, they knew they had something extraordinary: roughly 40 percent of a single skeleton, an almost unheard-of completeness for a fossil that old. That night, as the Beatles' "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds" played on a camp tape deck, someone gave the find its name.

Lucy, as she came to be known worldwide, was a member of the species Australopithecus afarensis. She stood just under four feet tall, had a brain about the size of a chimpanzee's, and died around 3.2 million years ago near what is now the village of Hadar. But what made her famous wasn't her age or her size. It was what her bones revealed about how she moved.
