The Fish That Learned to Walk Had Limbs Nobody Expected

In 2004, scientists found a 375-million-year-old fossil in the Arctic that preserved the exact moment fish began their invasion of land—complete with appendages that were neither fins nor feet, but something stranger.

Neil Shubin had a problem. The University of Chicago paleontologist needed to find a fossil that showed how fish first crawled onto land—one of the most important transitions in the history of life. He knew roughly when it should have happened: around 375 million years ago, during the Late Devonian period. He knew what kind of rock would preserve it: freshwater sediments from that era. What he needed was a place where those exact rocks were exposed at the surface, accessible to researchers with pickaxes and patience.

He found his answer in a geology textbook. The Canadian Arctic, specifically Ellesmere Island in Nunavut, had vast expanses of exposed Devonian rock. It was remote, expensive to reach, and frozen most of the year. But the rocks were the right age. In 2004, after three expeditions over six years, Shubin's team finally cracked open a slab of limestone and found what they'd been hunting: a flat-headed creature with scales like a fish, but shoulders and a neck like nothing the ocean had ever produced.
