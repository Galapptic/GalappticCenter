The Glue That Seals Wounds in Seconds Then Vanishes

Surgeons now close incisions with adhesives stronger than the tissue itself, yet these synthetic glues break down and disappear precisely when the body no longer needs them.

Stitches have held human flesh together for at least 5,000 years. Egyptian medical papyri describe suturing wounds with linen thread. Surgeons in ancient India used the heads of giant ants, clamping their mandibles onto wound edges before snapping off their bodies. For millennia, the basic principle stayed the same: pierce the tissue, pull a thread through, tie it off. The technique works, but it's slow, leaves needle holes that can scar, and creates tension points where bacteria can invade. In the operating room, closing a surgical incision with traditional sutures can take thirty minutes or more.

Today, a growing number of surgeons reach for something else entirely: surgical glue. These adhesives bond tissue in seconds, seal wounds without puncturing surrounding flesh, and then quietly dissolve once healing is complete. The technology represents one of the most significant shifts in wound closure since Joseph Lister introduced antiseptic techniques in the 1860s.
