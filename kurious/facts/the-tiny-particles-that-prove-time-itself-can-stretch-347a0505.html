The Tiny Particles That Prove Time Itself Can Stretch

Subatomic particles that should disintegrate long before reaching Earth's surface arrive by the billions every second—because for them, time runs slower than it does for us.

Every second, roughly 10,000 muons strike every square meter of Earth's surface. They're slamming into you right now—harmlessly passing through your body, the floor beneath you, and deep into the rock below. This is unremarkable until you do the math. Muons are created when cosmic rays collide with atoms in the upper atmosphere, about 15 kilometers up. They travel at nearly the speed of light. And they have an average lifetime of 2.2 microseconds. Multiply those numbers together and muons should travel about 660 meters before decaying. Not 15,000.

The muons shouldn't reach us. Yet they do, in enormous quantities. For decades, this simple observation has served as one of the most elegant proofs that Einstein was right: time does not tick at the same rate for everyone. What feels like 2.2 microseconds to a muon is considerably longer from our perspective on the ground. The universe, it turns out, has a flexible relationship with time.
