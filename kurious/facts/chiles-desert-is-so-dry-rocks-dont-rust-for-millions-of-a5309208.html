Chile's Desert Is So Dry Rocks Don't Rust for Millions of Years

In the Atacama Desert, iron-bearing rocks sit exposed for four million years without developing a speck of rust—making Earth's driest place a time capsule of geological stillness.

Rust needs water. Even the tiniest bit of moisture will eventually oxidize iron in rocks, turning them that familiar reddish-brown. But in Chile's Atacama Desert, some areas are so impossibly dry that iron-bearing rocks have sat on the surface for four million years without rusting. That's not a typo—four million years of near-perfect preservation. Some weather stations in the Atacama have never recorded rain. The average annual rainfall in the driest zones measures in fractions of a millimeter. This extreme aridity creates conditions so stable that geological processes essentially pause, leaving rocks frozen in time while the rest of the world weathers away.
