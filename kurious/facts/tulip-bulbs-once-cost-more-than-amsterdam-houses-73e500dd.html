Tulip Bulbs Once Cost More Than Amsterdam Houses

In 1637, a single tulip bulb sold for enough money to buy a luxurious canal house in Amsterdam, complete with a coach and garden.

During the Dutch Golden Age, the Netherlands went absolutely mad for tulips. These exotic flowers, originally from the Ottoman Empire, became status symbols among wealthy merchants. By the mid-1630s, prices had spiraled beyond all reason. A single Semper Augustus bulb—famous for its striking red and white flames—reportedly sold for 10,000 guilders. That's roughly what a skilled craftsman would earn in an entire decade. People mortgaged homes, sold businesses, and traded livestock for bulbs. At the peak, tulip futures were changing hands in taverns like stock trades. Then, in February 1637, buyers simply stopped showing up to auctions. Prices collapsed within days, leaving speculators ruined and creating history's most famous market bubble.
