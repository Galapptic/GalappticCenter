Your Home Is Your Castle and the Law Might Let You Kill to Defend It

A legal doctrine born in 17th-century English courts now lets Americans shoot intruders dead without any obligation to run away first.

In 1604, the English jurist Sir Edward Coke wrote that "the house of every one is to him as his castle and fortress, as well for his defence against injury and violence as for his repose." He was explaining why a man who killed someone breaking into his home could not be convicted of murder. Four centuries later, his words still echo through American courtrooms, where the Castle Doctrine grants homeowners extraordinary legal protection when they use deadly force against intruders.

The doctrine represents one of the sharpest exceptions to the general rule that taking a human life is a crime. In most situations, even when acting in self-defense, Americans have a legal duty to retreat—to run away if possible rather than kill. But step across someone's threshold uninvited, and that calculus changes dramatically. The homeowner's duty to flee vanishes, replaced by the right to stand and fight.
