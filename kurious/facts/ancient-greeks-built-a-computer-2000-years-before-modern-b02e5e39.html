Ancient Greeks Built a Computer 2000 Years Before Modern Ones

Discovered in a shipwreck, this corroded bronze device contained more than 30 precision gears that could predict eclipses and planetary positions with stunning accuracy.

In 1901, sponge divers exploring a Roman-era shipwreck near the Greek island of Antikythera pulled up a corroded lump of bronze that would baffle scientists for decades. What looked like fused metal fragments turned out to be the Antikythera Mechanism—the world's oldest known analog computer, built around 100 BCE. This shoebox-sized device used an intricate system of interlocking bronze gears to calculate astronomical positions and predict celestial events. Users could turn a hand crank to display the positions of the Sun, Moon, and five known planets across the sky. It even predicted lunar and solar eclipses years in advance.
