The Spinning Needle That Reads Vinyl Records Without Ever Touching Them

A laser beam, spinning at 1,000 rotations per minute, can resurrect the voices of the dead from cracked and broken records that no stylus could ever play again.

In the basement of the Library of Congress, archivists face a peculiar problem. Their vaults hold some of the rarest sound recordings in existence—wax cylinders from the 1890s, shellac discs from the jazz age, acetate masters that captured voices now silent for a century. Many are cracked, warped, or so fragile that dropping a needle into their grooves would destroy them forever. For decades, these recordings sat in climate-controlled silence, their sounds locked away like messages in bottles no one dared open.

Then, in 2002, a team of physicists at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory unveiled something remarkable: a turntable with no needle at all. Instead, it used light itself to read the groove—a spinning laser that could extract sound from the most damaged records without ever making contact. They called it IRENE, and it would go on to resurrect voices that history had nearly lost.
