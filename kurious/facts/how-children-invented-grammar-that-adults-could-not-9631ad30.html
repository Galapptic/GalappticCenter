How Children Invented Grammar That Adults Could Not

In 1980s Nicaragua, deaf children left in a schoolyard created a fully grammatical language in a single generation—proving that human brains come factory-equipped to build linguistic rules from nothing.

The Sandinista revolution of 1979 brought many changes to Nicaragua, but one of the strangest happened almost by accident. The new government opened the country's first schools for deaf children, gathering hundreds of kids who had never met another deaf person. They arrived with only the crude home signs they'd invented with their families—gestures for 'eat,' 'sleep,' 'come here.' What happened next would overturn long-held assumptions about how languages are born.

Within a few years, these children had spontaneously created Nicaraguan Sign Language, a fully grammatical system with verb conjugations, spatial markers for time, and ways to embed one clause inside another. They did it without teachers, without textbooks, without any model to copy. Linguists who studied them realized they were watching something that normally takes centuries happen in real time: the birth of a language from grammatical scratch.
