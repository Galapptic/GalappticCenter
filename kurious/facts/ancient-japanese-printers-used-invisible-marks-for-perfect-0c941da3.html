Ancient Japanese Printers Used Invisible Marks for Perfect Color

Centuries before laser alignment technology, Japanese artisans achieved microscopic color precision using marks designed to disappear in the final print.

Creating a full-color woodblock print requires running the same sheet of paper through multiple carved blocks—sometimes ten or more—each applying a different color. If any block is even slightly misaligned, colors bleed outside their boundaries, ruining the image. Japanese ukiyo-e printers solved this problem with 'kento,' small L-shaped marks carved into the corner and edge of every block. The printer would align the paper against these identical marks on each block, ensuring perfect registration every time. These guides were positioned in areas that would be trimmed away or hidden in margins. The system was so precise that colors could meet perfectly without overlap, creating the crisp, vibrant images that made Japanese prints famous worldwide.
