Doctors Drained Your Blood for 3,000 Years and Called It Medicine

From ancient Egypt to Victorian England, the most trusted cure in medicine was to slice open a vein and let you bleed—and physicians swore it worked for everything from acne to the plague.

In 1799, George Washington woke with a sore throat. Over the next twelve hours, his physicians drained nearly half the blood in his body—about 2.4 liters. They were following the most respected medical protocol of their era. Washington died that evening. His doctors believed they simply hadn't acted fast enough. This was bloodletting: for three millennia, the most universal treatment in medicine, prescribed for infections, fevers, madness, and heartbreak alike.
