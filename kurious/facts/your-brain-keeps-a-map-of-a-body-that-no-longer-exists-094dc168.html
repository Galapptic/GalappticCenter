Your Brain Keeps a Map of a Body That No Longer Exists

Decades after losing a limb, amputees can feel their missing fingers being crushed one by one—because the brain refuses to delete its blueprint of the body.

In 1866, a Philadelphia physician named Silas Weir Mitchell sat in a hospital overflowing with Civil War amputees. The soldiers told him something strange: they could still feel their missing arms and legs. Not just a vague sense that something was there, but specific sensations—itching in a thumb that had been buried at Gettysburg, cramping in toes that no longer existed. Mitchell coined the term "phantom limb," and for the next century, medicine had no good explanation for why the brain keeps talking to body parts that are gone.
