The Blonde Mummies of China's Desert

In the 1990s, archaeologists opened graves in one of Earth's most forbidding deserts and found 4,000-year-old bodies with fair hair, pale skin, and European features—2,000 miles from Europe.

The corpses lay in the sand like sleepers. Their skin was leathery but intact. Their hair—reddish-brown, blonde, auburn—still clung to their skulls. Some wore felt hats and woolen cloaks. One woman had been buried in a conical hat that looked almost witchlike. Another man had tattoos on his face. They died between 2,000 and 4,000 years ago, in what is now China's Xinjiang region, and the hyper-arid conditions of the Taklamakan Desert preserved them almost perfectly.

When researchers examined the mummies closely, they found something startling: these people didn't look Chinese. They had deep-set eyes, long noses, and features that appeared distinctly Western. DNA analysis confirmed it. These were not the ancestors of modern East Asians. They were something else entirely—and their discovery rewrote our understanding of ancient migration.
