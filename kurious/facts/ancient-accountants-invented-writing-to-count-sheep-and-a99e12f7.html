Ancient Accountants Invented Writing to Count Sheep and Grain

The world's first writers weren't poets or priests—they were bureaucrats obsessing over barley shipments.

Around 3400 BCE in ancient Sumeria, someone needed to track how much grain was going into storage and how much was coming out. The solution? Press a reed stylus into wet clay and make marks. These early symbols weren't love letters or epic tales—they were spreadsheets. Each wedge-shaped impression represented quantities of barley, livestock, or beer rations for workers. For centuries, writing remained purely administrative. Temples and palaces used it to record debts, wages, and inventories. The very first 'documents' in human history were essentially receipts. Literature, religion, and personal expression came much later, hitchhiking on a system designed for the deeply unsexy purpose of preventing accountants from losing track of sheep.
