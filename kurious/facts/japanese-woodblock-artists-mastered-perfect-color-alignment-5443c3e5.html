Japanese Woodblock Artists Mastered Perfect Color Alignment Centuries Ago

While Europe struggled with smudgy single-color prints, Japanese craftsmen were producing flawless multi-color images using nothing but carved wood, water-based inks, and extraordinary precision.

In 18th-century Japan, woodblock printers achieved something remarkable: perfect color registration across dozens of separate printing blocks. Each color in a traditional ukiyo-e print required its own carved wooden block, and every block had to align precisely with the others. A single print might need ten to twenty separate impressions, each landing exactly where intended. The secret was the kento system—small carved notches on each block that guided paper placement with millimeter accuracy. Printers worked entirely by hand and eye, yet produced results that rival modern industrial printing. This wasn't just craftsmanship; it was engineering disguised as art, developed centuries before Western color printing existed.
