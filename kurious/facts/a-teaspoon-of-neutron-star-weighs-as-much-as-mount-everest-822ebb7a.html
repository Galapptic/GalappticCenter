A Teaspoon of Neutron Star Weighs as Much as Mount Everest

When a massive star dies, its core collapses into something so impossibly dense that a sugar-cube-sized chunk would outweigh every car on Earth.

In 1934, just two years after the neutron was discovered, astronomers Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky proposed something extraordinary: that when giant stars explode, they leave behind ultra-compact remnants made almost entirely of neutrons. Their colleagues were skeptical. The numbers seemed absurd. But Baade and Zwicky were right, and the reality turned out to be even stranger than they imagined.

A neutron star packs roughly 1.4 times the mass of our Sun into a sphere just 20 kilometers across—about the length of Manhattan. At that compression, a teaspoon of neutron star material weighs around 6 billion tons, roughly equal to Mount Everest. Drop that teaspoon on Earth and it would punch straight through the crust like a bullet through tissue paper.
