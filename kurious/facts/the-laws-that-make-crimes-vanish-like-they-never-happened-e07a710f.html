The Laws That Make Crimes Vanish Like They Never Happened

In dozens of countries, a single piece of legislation can legally transform convicted murderers, torturers, and war criminals into people with spotless records who can truthfully say they committed no crime.

On a spring morning in 1977, Juan María Bordaberry walked out of a Uruguayan courtroom a free man. He had presided over a dictatorship that imprisoned thousands, authorized torture, and dissolved the country's parliament. But none of that appeared on his record anymore. An amnesty law had wiped it clean—not pardoned it, not forgiven it, but erased it. Under the law, those crimes had never occurred. Bordaberry wasn't a criminal granted mercy. He was, legally speaking, a man who had done nothing wrong.

This is the strange and troubling power of amnesty laws: they don't just forgive crimes, they unmake them. The distinction matters enormously. A pardon acknowledges guilt but removes punishment. An amnesty reaches backward through time and declares that the offense itself never existed. The convicted can apply for jobs, run for office, and answer 'no' when asked if they've ever committed a crime—because in the eyes of the law, they haven't.
