Black Garlic Gets Its Sweetness From a Slow Chemical Trick

Raw garlic's pungent bite transforms into something resembling balsamic vinegar and molasses—but no microbes are involved.

Crack open a bulb of black garlic and you'll find cloves the color of squid ink, soft as dates, tasting of caramel and soy sauce. It's become a darling of restaurant kitchens since Korean producers began exporting it widely in the early 2000s. But the process that creates it isn't really fermentation at all—it's something older and stranger, driven entirely by heat, humidity, and time.
