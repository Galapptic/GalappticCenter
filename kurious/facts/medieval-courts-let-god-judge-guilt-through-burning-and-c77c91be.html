Medieval Courts Let God Judge Guilt Through Burning and Drowning

If you survived being thrown into freezing water or holding red-hot iron, medieval law declared you innocent — because God must have intervened.

Before modern evidence and forensic science, medieval European courts faced a problem: how do you determine guilt when witnesses lie and evidence is scarce? Their solution was elegantly brutal — let God decide. Trial by ordeal forced accused persons to endure painful tests, believing divine intervention would protect the innocent. The most common ordeals involved fire and water. In the ordeal of hot iron, the accused gripped a glowing metal bar and walked several paces. Their hand was then bandaged for three days. If it healed cleanly, God had declared them innocent. If it festered, guilt was proven. Water ordeals worked similarly — either plunging hands into boiling water to retrieve stones, or being bound and thrown into cold water, where sinking meant innocence.
