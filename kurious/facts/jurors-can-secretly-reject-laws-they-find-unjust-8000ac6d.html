Jurors Can Secretly Reject Laws They Find Unjust

Every juror in America holds a hidden power that judges won't tell them about—the ability to acquit someone they believe is guilty if they think the law itself is wrong.

When you sit on a jury, you're told to judge the facts—did the defendant do it or not? But there's something judges deliberately don't mention: you can also judge the law itself. Jury nullification occurs when jurors acquit a defendant they believe is technically guilty because they consider the law unjust or wrongly applied. Since jury deliberations are secret and jurors can't be punished for their verdicts, this power is effectively unstoppable. Courts won't instruct jurors about this right, and defense attorneys can be held in contempt for mentioning it. Yet it remains embedded in the system, a democratic safety valve hiding in plain sight within every courtroom.
