A Debt of Eight Dollars Gave Us the Safety Pin

Walter Hunt owed a draftsman fifteen dollars and paid it off by inventing something in three hours that would eventually fasten billions of diapers, uniforms, and punk rock jackets.

On a spring afternoon in 1849, Walter Hunt sat in his New York City workshop twisting a piece of brass wire between his fingers. He wasn't trying to invent anything. He was fidgeting, the way a nervous man does when he owes money he doesn't have. The man across from him, a draftsman named J.R. Chapin, had come to collect on a fifteen-dollar debt—about five hundred dollars today. Hunt had nothing to give him but his hands and whatever ideas might fall out of his restless mind.

Within three hours, Hunt had bent that wire into a clasp with a coiled spring at one end and a protective sheath at the other. The point tucked safely inside, unable to poke the wearer. He called it a 'dress pin.' Chapin, recognizing its potential, offered Hunt a deal: he would forgive the debt and pay Hunt an additional four hundred dollars if Hunt signed over the patent rights. Hunt agreed on the spot. It was, by any measure, a terrible bargain—and one of the most consequential acts of fidgeting in mechanical history.
