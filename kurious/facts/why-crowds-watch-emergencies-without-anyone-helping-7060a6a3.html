Why Crowds Watch Emergencies Without Anyone Helping

The more people witness an emergency, the less likely any single person is to help — a tragic paradox that cost Kitty Genovese her life.

Picture someone collapsing on a busy street. You'd think with dozens of witnesses, help would arrive instantly. But here's the dark twist: the more people present, the less likely anyone helps. This is the bystander effect, and it's disturbingly reliable. Each person assumes someone else will call for help or step in. We look around, see others standing still, and unconsciously conclude the situation must not be that serious. Our brain essentially crowdsources the decision to act — and when everyone's waiting for someone else to move first, nobody moves at all. It's not that people are cruel; it's that responsibility evaporates when it's shared among many.
