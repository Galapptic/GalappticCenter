How Dead Judges Still Control the Courts That Replaced Them

Every day, courts around the world are legally obligated to follow rulings made by judges who died centuries ago—and the system was basically an accident.

In 2023, an English judge ruled that a man could not sue his neighbor over a property boundary. The reason? A decision made in 1834. The judge who wrote that original ruling had been dead for nearly two centuries, yet his words carried the force of law. This wasn't unusual. It was stare decisis at work—the doctrine that courts must follow past decisions, even when the judges who made them are long gone and the world has utterly transformed.

Stare decisis is Latin for "to stand by things decided." It's the backbone of common law systems in Britain, the United States, Canada, Australia, and dozens of other countries. But here's what most people don't realize: nobody actually designed it this way. Courts stumbled into this system of binding precedent, and by the time anyone thought to question it, they were already trapped.
