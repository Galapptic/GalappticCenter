Zombie Planets Orbit the Corpses of Dead Stars

The first exoplanets ever discovered weren't circling vibrant suns—they were orbiting a rapidly spinning stellar corpse that blasts radiation across the cosmos.

When massive stars die, they don't always take their planetary systems with them. Some leave behind pulsars—rapidly spinning neutron stars that emit beams of radiation like cosmic lighthouses. Remarkably, planets can orbit these stellar remnants, creating what astronomers call cosmic graveyards. These worlds endure extreme conditions: bathed in intense radiation, experiencing gravitational forces that would crush anything familiar. The first confirmed exoplanets ever discovered weren't found around a normal star like our Sun. In 1992, astronomers detected planets orbiting a pulsar called PSR B1257+12, located about 2,300 light-years away. This discovery revolutionized our understanding of where planets can exist, proving that worlds can survive—or even form—in the aftermath of stellar death.
