The First Thermometers Had No Numbers at All

For nearly a century, scientists measured heat with unmarked tubes of colored alcohol, agreeing on nothing—not even whether water boiled at 0 or 212.

In 1593, Galileo Galilei built a device that could detect changes in temperature. It was a glass tube with a bulb at one end, inverted into a dish of water. When the air in the bulb warmed, it expanded and pushed the water down; when it cooled, the water rose. It worked, more or less. But there were no numbers on it, no scale, no way to compare one reading to another. Galileo had invented something that showed temperature was changing without being able to say what temperature actually was.

This was the thermometer's awkward beginning: a tool that detected heat but couldn't measure it. For nearly a hundred years afterward, natural philosophers across Europe tinkered with sealed tubes of wine, alcohol, and eventually mercury, each inventor choosing their own reference points, their own scales, their own numbers. The result was chaos. A temperature recorded in Florence meant nothing in London. The thermometer existed, but temperature as a universal concept did not.
