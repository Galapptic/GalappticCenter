Dutch Flower Bulbs Once Cost More Than Luxury Mansions

At the peak of Tulip Mania in 1637, a single tulip bulb sold for enough money to buy a grand canal house in Amsterdam.

In the 1630s Netherlands, something bizarre happened. Ordinary people began trading tulip bulbs for astronomical sums. At the market's peak in February 1637, a single Semper Augustus bulb—prized for its flame-like red and white streaks—reportedly sold for 10,000 guilders. For context, a skilled craftsman earned about 300 guilders yearly, and a nice Amsterdam canal house cost around 5,000 guilders. People mortgaged homes, sold businesses, and traded livestock for bulbs they'd never even plant. Then, almost overnight, prices collapsed. The bubble burst at a Haarlem auction when nobody showed up to buy. Within weeks, bulbs worth fortunes became nearly worthless, leaving speculators ruined.
