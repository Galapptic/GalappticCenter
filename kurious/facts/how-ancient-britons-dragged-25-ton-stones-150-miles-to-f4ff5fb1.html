How Ancient Britons Dragged 25-Ton Stones 150 Miles to Stonehenge

The builders of Stonehenge transported rocks heavier than a school bus across Wales using nothing but ropes, sledges, and an understanding of physics that would impress modern engineers.

Stonehenge sits on Salisbury Plain in southern England, a circle of massive stones that has baffled visitors for centuries. The largest uprights, called sarsens, weigh up to 25 tons each. The smaller bluestones, some weighing 4 tons, came from quarries in Wales—150 miles away. The monument took shape between roughly 3000 and 2000 BCE, built by people who had no wheels capable of bearing such loads, no draft animals strong enough to pull them, and no metal tools harder than copper. Yet they moved these rocks, shaped them, and raised them into positions so precise that some alignments still mark the summer solstice sunrise.
