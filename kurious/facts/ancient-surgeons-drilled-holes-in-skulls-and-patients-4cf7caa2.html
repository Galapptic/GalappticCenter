Ancient Surgeons Drilled Holes in Skulls and Patients Survived

Archaeological evidence shows that up to 90% of trepanation patients in some ancient cultures survived the procedure—without anesthesia, antibiotics, or sterile environments.

Trepanation—deliberately cutting or drilling holes into the skull—is humanity's oldest known surgical procedure, dating back over 7,000 years. Ancient surgeons used stone tools, obsidian blades, and later metal instruments to carefully remove circular sections of bone from living patients. The shocking part? Many survived. We know this because their skulls show clear signs of bone regrowth around the holes, proving they lived months or even years after surgery. This wasn't a rare practice either—thousands of trepanned skulls have been found across every inhabited continent. From Neolithic Europe to pre-Columbian Peru, from ancient China to medieval England, cultures independently developed this technique, suggesting it provided some perceived medical benefit worth the terrifying risk.
