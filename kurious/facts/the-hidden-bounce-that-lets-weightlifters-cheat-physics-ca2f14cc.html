The Hidden Bounce That Lets Weightlifters Cheat Physics

Elite lifters can hoist more weight than their muscles can actually support—by exploiting a neurological quirk that turns their tendons into loaded springs.

In 1988, Bulgarian weightlifter Naim Süleymanoğlu stood just 4 feet 11 inches tall and weighed 132 pounds. He clean and jerked 419 pounds—more than three times his body weight. The bar bent around his shoulders. His face turned crimson. And for a fraction of a second, his muscles weren't doing most of the work. His tendons were.

Süleymanoğlu, nicknamed the 'Pocket Hercules,' was a master of timing. Like all elite weightlifters, he understood something that most gym-goers never learn: the human body has a built-in amplifier, a neurological reflex that can add roughly 10 to 15 percent to any explosive lift. It's called the stretch reflex, and without it, some of the most iconic lifts in sports history would have been physically impossible.
