The Tiny Titanium Clips That Squeeze Deadly Brain Bulges Shut

A device smaller than a fingernail, clamped permanently onto a pulsing artery inside your skull, is often the only thing standing between a patient and catastrophic brain hemorrhage.

Somewhere in the world, roughly every eighteen minutes, a brain aneurysm ruptures. The thin-walled bulge on a cerebral artery gives way, and blood floods into the surrounding tissue. About 40 percent of these patients die. Many who survive face permanent disability. Yet for decades, neurosurgeons have possessed a remarkably simple countermeasure: a spring-loaded clip, often made of titanium, that pinches the weak spot shut and stays there for life.

These aneurysm clips look almost comically mundane—like tiny hair clips or miniature clothespins. But their design represents one of the most precise balancing acts in surgical engineering. Too weak, and they slip off a pulsing artery. Too strong, and they shear through the vessel wall. The clips must grip with exactly enough force to seal the aneurysm without damaging the healthy tissue, then maintain that pressure for fifty years or more inside one of the most delicate environments in the human body.
