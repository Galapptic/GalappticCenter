Bloodsucking Leeches Are Back in Modern Operating Rooms

The same creatures medieval doctors used for everything from headaches to hysteria now help save severed fingers and reattached ears in cutting-edge microsurgery.

For centuries, leeches symbolized everything wrong with pre-scientific medicine. Doctors slapped them on patients for virtually any ailment, often doing more harm than good. By the twentieth century, leech therapy seemed as outdated as bloodletting itself. Then something unexpected happened. Plastic surgeons in the 1980s rediscovered these annelid worms had a genuine medical superpower. When reattaching severed fingers, ears, or scalps, surgeons face a critical problem: tiny veins often fail to reconnect properly, causing dangerous blood pooling. Leeches solve this brilliantly. They drain excess blood while their saliva delivers nature's most effective anticoagulant cocktail, keeping blood flowing until the body rebuilds its own circulation. The FDA officially approved medicinal leeches in 2004.
