The Iceman Cometh: What a Frozen Corpse Taught Us About Ourselves

A German couple hiking in the Alps in 1991 stumbled upon a body so well preserved they assumed it was a recent murder victim—it turned out to be 5,300 years old.

On September 19, 1991, Helmut and Erika Simon were descending from the Fineilspitze peak in the Ötztal Alps, near the Austrian-Italian border. At 3,210 meters elevation, they noticed something dark poking through the melting ice. It looked human. They photographed what they thought was the corpse of an unlucky mountaineer and alerted authorities. Within days, the body was chiseled out with ice axes and a pneumatic drill—methods that would horrify archaeologists—and airlifted to the University of Innsbruck.

Initial estimates put the body's age at a few hundred years. Then radiocarbon dating came back: the man had died between 3350 and 3100 BCE. He was older than the Egyptian pyramids, older than Stonehenge's stone circle, older than the earliest known writing. And unlike skeletal remains or wrapped mummies, his skin, organs, hair, and final meal were intact. Researchers named him Ötzi, after the Ötztal valley where he emerged from the ice.
