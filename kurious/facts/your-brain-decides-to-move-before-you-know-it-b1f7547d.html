Your Brain Decides to Move Before You Know It

Three hundred and fifty milliseconds before you reach for your coffee, your brain has already committed to the action—raising unsettling questions about whether free will is an illusion.

In 1965, two German scientists attached electrodes to people's scalps and asked them to do something simple: move a finger whenever they felt like it. What Hans Helmut Kornhuber and Lüder Deecke discovered would eventually shake the foundations of philosophy, neuroscience, and our most basic assumptions about human agency. They found a slow electrical buildup in the brain—a kind of neural runway—that began nearly a full second before the finger actually moved. They called it the Bereitschaftspotential, or readiness potential.

This wave of electrical activity, ramping up in the motor regions of the brain, seemed to suggest something profound: the brain starts preparing for action long before we consciously decide to act. The discovery sat quietly in the scientific literature for years, a curiosity waiting for someone to ask the obvious, uncomfortable question.
