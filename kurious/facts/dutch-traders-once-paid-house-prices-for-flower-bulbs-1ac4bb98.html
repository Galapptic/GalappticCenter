Dutch Traders Once Paid House Prices for Flower Bulbs

At the peak of tulip mania in 1637, a single Semper Augustus bulb sold for enough money to buy the grandest canal house in Amsterdam.

In the 1630s, the Dutch Republic went absolutely mad for tulips. These exotic flowers, originally from the Ottoman Empire, became status symbols among wealthy merchants. Prices climbed steadily, then explosively. At the fever pitch in early 1637, rare bulb varieties changed hands for staggering sums. The most coveted Semper Augustus bulb reportedly sold for 10,000 guilders—when a skilled craftsman earned about 300 guilders yearly. That same sum could purchase a luxurious Amsterdam canal house with a coach house and garden. People mortgaged homes and businesses to speculate on bulbs they'd never even plant. Then, in February 1637, prices collapsed almost overnight.
