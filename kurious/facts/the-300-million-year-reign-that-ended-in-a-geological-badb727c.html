The 300-Million-Year Reign That Ended in a Geological Instant

For longer than dinosaurs, mammals, and flowering plants combined have existed, one creature ruled Earth's oceans—then vanished so completely that we forgot they ever existed until a child found one in 1698.

In the summer of 1698, a young boy named Edward Lhwyd found a strange stone in Wales. It had the shape of a flattened insect, with a segmented body and what looked like compound eyes staring up from the rock. Lhwyd, who would grow up to become a naturalist, sketched it carefully and called it a 'flatfish skeleton.' He had no way of knowing he was looking at an animal older than the Atlantic Ocean—a creature whose ancestors had been swimming when the nearest landmass to Wales was Antarctica.

What Lhwyd found was a trilobite. For 300 million years—from roughly 521 to 252 million years ago—these arthropods dominated Earth's oceans with a diversity that puts modern insects to shame. They evolved into more than 20,000 known species across every marine environment on the planet. Then, at the end of the Permian period, they disappeared entirely. Not a single species survived. It remains one of the most complete erasures in the history of life.
