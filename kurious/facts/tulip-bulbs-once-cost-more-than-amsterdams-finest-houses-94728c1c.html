Tulip Bulbs Once Cost More Than Amsterdam's Finest Houses

At the peak of 1637's tulip mania, a single bulb sold for enough money to buy a grand canal house in Amsterdam.

In the 1630s, the Dutch Republic went absolutely mad for tulips. These exotic flowers, originally from the Ottoman Empire, became status symbols among the wealthy merchant class. Prices climbed steadily, then exploded. At the frenzy's peak in February 1637, a single Semper Augustus bulb—with its distinctive flame-like red streaks on white petals—sold for 10,000 guilders. For context, a skilled craftsman earned about 300 guilders per year, and a nice Amsterdam canal house cost around 5,000 guilders. People mortgaged their homes, sold their businesses, and traded their life savings for bulbs that weighed less than an onion. Then, almost overnight, the market collapsed.
