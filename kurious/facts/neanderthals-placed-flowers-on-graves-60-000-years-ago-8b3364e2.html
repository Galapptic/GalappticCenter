Neanderthals Placed Flowers on Graves 60,000 Years Ago

Long before modern humans invented cemeteries, our ancient cousins were decorating their dead with carefully arranged wildflowers.

In 1960, archaeologists excavating Shanidar Cave in Iraqi Kurdistan made a stunning discovery. Among Neanderthal remains dating back 60,000 years, they found concentrated clusters of ancient pollen—evidence that someone had deliberately placed flowers around the bodies. This wasn't random wind-blown pollen; the clusters suggested intentional bouquets of colorful wildflowers including yarrow, cornflowers, and hollyhocks. The finding shattered assumptions that Neanderthals were brutish and unthinking. Here was evidence of ritual, symbolism, and perhaps grief. These ancient humans apparently understood death as something meaningful, worthy of ceremony and beauty. The flower burials suggested Neanderthals possessed emotional depth we once thought uniquely human.
