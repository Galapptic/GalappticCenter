Flying Buttresses Don't Hold Up Walls—They Hold Them Down

The soaring stone arches of Gothic cathedrals aren't pushing against gravity—they're pushing against the building's own tendency to explode outward.

Walk past any Gothic cathedral and you'll see them: those graceful stone arms reaching from outer piers to the main walls, arching through empty air like the ribs of some enormous stone creature. They look decorative, almost unnecessary—afterthoughts tacked onto buildings that could probably stand without them. This is exactly wrong. Without flying buttresses, the walls of Notre-Dame would have blown apart eight centuries ago.

The confusion is understandable. We think of architecture as a battle against gravity, a constant struggle to keep heavy things from falling down. But Gothic cathedrals face a different enemy entirely. Their walls don't want to collapse inward—they want to burst outward, pushed by the very roofs they support. Flying buttresses are the solution: not props holding walls up, but anchors holding them in.
