Volcanic Ash Preserved Roman Bread and Graffiti for 2000 Years

Ancient Romans scratched crude jokes and political slogans onto walls that would survive longer than their empire itself.

In 79 AD, Mount Vesuvius erupted with catastrophic force, burying the Roman city of Pompeii under roughly 20 feet of volcanic ash and pumice. But this destruction created something extraordinary: a near-perfect time capsule. The ash fell so quickly and sealed so completely that it preserved organic materials that normally decay within years. Archaeologists have found intact loaves of bread still sitting in ovens, complete with baker's stamps. Wooden furniture, food, and even the shapes of human bodies were captured in the moment of death. Perhaps most fascinating are the graffiti—thousands of messages scratched into walls revealing everyday Roman life, from love declarations to election slogans to bathroom humor.
