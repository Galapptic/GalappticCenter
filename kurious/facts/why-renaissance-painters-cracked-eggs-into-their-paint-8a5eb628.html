Why Renaissance Painters Cracked Eggs Into Their Paint

The secret ingredient that let Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling survive five centuries wasn't some exotic pigment from the Far East—it was the same thing you scramble for breakfast.

When Cennino Cennini wrote his painter's handbook around 1400, he devoted careful attention to eggs. Not as food, but as chemistry. A medieval painter buying pigments—ground lapis lazuli from Afghanistan, red ochre from Tuscany, bone black made from charred animal bones—faced a basic problem: colored powder doesn't stick to anything. It needs a binder, something to glue it to a surface. For Italian painters working on wooden panels and wet plaster walls, that binder came from the henhouse.
