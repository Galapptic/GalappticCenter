Cave Sculptures Grow Slower Than Your Fingernails Ever Could

A single stalactite hanging in your local cave might have started forming when woolly mammoths still roamed the Earth.

Every stalactite and stalagmite you've ever marveled at in a cave was built one microscopic mineral deposit at a time. When water seeps through limestone bedrock, it dissolves calcium carbonate along the way. As each droplet reaches a cave ceiling, it hangs briefly before falling. In that moment, a tiny amount of carbon dioxide escapes into the cave air, forcing the water to leave behind an infinitesimal ring of mineral. Over thousands of years, these rings stack into the icicle-like formations we call stalactites. The drops that hit the floor build stalagmites upward. Growth rates average just 0.1 to 0.3 millimeters per year—meaning a one-inch formation might be 250 years old.
