Octopuses Run a Distributed Brain Network Across Their Body

Two-thirds of an octopus's neurons live in its arms, which can literally taste, think, and act independently of the central brain.

When scientists say octopuses have nine brains, they're describing something genuinely weird. There's one central brain wrapped around the esophagus, plus eight smaller neural clusters called ganglia—one controlling each arm. These aren't just relay stations. Each arm ganglion contains about 40 million neurons and can process sensory information, make decisions, and execute complex movements without consulting headquarters. If you sever an arm from an octopus, it continues behaving purposefully for hours—reaching, grasping, even passing food toward where the mouth used to be. Meanwhile, those same arms are covered in hundreds of suckers, each packed with chemoreceptors. An octopus doesn't just touch its environment; it tastes everything it contacts.
