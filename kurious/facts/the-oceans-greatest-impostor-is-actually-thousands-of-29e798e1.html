The Ocean's Greatest Impostor Is Actually Thousands of Animals

What looks like a single jellyfish drifting through the deep ocean is actually a colony of thousands of specialized organisms so committed to their shared identity that they've fused their bodies together and can never survive apart.

In 1758, when Carl Linnaeus first classified the Portuguese man o' war, he placed it among the jellyfish. It looked like a jellyfish. It stung like a jellyfish. It drifted through the Atlantic like a jellyfish. He was completely wrong. What Linnaeus was looking at wasn't a single animal at all, but a floating colony of thousands of tiny organisms called zooids, each so specialized for its task that it had lost the ability to exist alone. Together, they form something that functions as a single creature—breathing, hunting, digesting, and reproducing as one.

These are siphonophores, and they represent one of evolution's strangest experiments in what it means to be an individual. They blur the line between organism and colony so thoroughly that biologists still argue about how to classify them. Are they superorganisms? Colonial animals? Something entirely new? The answer matters less than the extraordinary biology that makes the question so difficult.
