The Liquid That Crawls Up Walls and Escapes Its Container

Cool helium to within two degrees of absolute zero and it transforms into a substance so bizarre it can flow uphill, squeeze through solid glass, and climb out of any cup you put it in.

In 1938, two physics labs on opposite sides of the world made the same impossible observation. At Cambridge, Jack Allen and Don Misener watched liquid helium creep up the sides of its container and spill over the rim. In Moscow, Pyotr Kapitsa saw it flow through gaps so narrow that no ordinary liquid could pass. Both teams had stumbled onto the same phenomenon: below a certain temperature, helium stops behaving like any liquid on Earth.

They had discovered superfluidity—a quantum state where billions of atoms move as one, friction vanishes entirely, and the normal rules of fluid dynamics simply stop applying. It remains one of the strangest things matter can do, and it only happens at temperatures colder than outer space.
