Hawaiian Language Has No Past or Future Tense Markers

In Hawaiian, you never grammatically traveled yesterday or will arrive tomorrow—every verb exists in an eternal now.

Hawaiian doesn't use verb conjugations to indicate when something happens. Unlike English, where "walked" signals past and "will walk" signals future, Hawaiian verbs remain unchanged regardless of time. Instead of tense markers, speakers use context clues like "i nehinei" (yesterday) or "apōpō" (tomorrow) to situate events temporally. The verb itself stays constant. This isn't a limitation—it's a fundamentally different approach to expressing time. Many linguists describe Hawaiian as "tenseless," meaning time isn't grammatically obligatory. Speakers choose when temporal information matters rather than forcing it into every sentence. This creates a fluid relationship with time that reflects deeper cultural perspectives about existence and experience.
