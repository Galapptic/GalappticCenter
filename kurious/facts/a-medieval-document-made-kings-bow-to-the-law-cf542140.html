A Medieval Document Made Kings Bow to the Law

In 1215, a group of angry barons forced England's most despised king to sign away his absolute power at swordpoint.

King John of England was a disaster. He lost wars, raised crushing taxes, and imprisoned people without trial. By 1215, his barons had enough. They marched an army to a meadow called Runnymede and gave John a choice: sign this charter or face civil war. The Magna Carta wasn't about democracy—it protected nobles from royal abuse. But buried in its Latin clauses was something revolutionary: the idea that even a king must follow rules. Clause 39 promised no free man would be imprisoned except by lawful judgment. Clause 40 declared the king couldn't sell or deny justice. For the first time, power had written limits.
