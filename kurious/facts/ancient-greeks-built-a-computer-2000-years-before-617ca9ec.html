Ancient Greeks Built a Computer 2000 Years Before Electronics

Discovered in a shipwreck, a corroded bronze device turned out to be an astronomical calculator so sophisticated it wouldn't be matched for over a millennium.

In 1901, sponge divers exploring a Roman-era shipwreck near the Greek island of Antikythera hauled up something extraordinary: a corroded lump of bronze gears. It took decades before researchers understood what they'd found. The Antikythera Mechanism, dating to around 100 BCE, is essentially an ancient Greek computer. This shoebox-sized device used at least 30 interlocking bronze gears to track the movements of the Sun, Moon, and planets. Users could input a date and predict eclipses, lunar phases, and even the timing of the ancient Olympic Games. Nothing this complex would appear again in the historical record for over a thousand years.
