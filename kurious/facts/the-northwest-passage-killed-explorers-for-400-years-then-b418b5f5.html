The Northwest Passage Killed Explorers for 400 Years Then Proved Useless

For four centuries, the world's greatest maritime powers sent expedition after expedition into the Arctic's frozen labyrinth, sacrificing hundreds of lives to find a shortcut that turned out to be commercially worthless.

In 1497, just five years after Columbus reached the Americas, John Cabot sailed west from England with a different goal: finding a northern sea route to Asia. He died trying. So would countless others over the next four centuries. The Northwest Passage—a hypothetical shipping lane threading through the Arctic islands north of Canada—became geography's cruelest obsession, luring nation after nation into a frozen maze that swallowed ships whole and left crews to starve on the ice.

When explorers finally navigated the passage in the twentieth century, they discovered something the ice had hidden all along: the route was too shallow, too frozen, and too dangerous to replace the long voyage around Cape Horn. The greatest prize in exploration was, commercially speaking, fool's gold.
