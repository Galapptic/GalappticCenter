Why Mathematicians Study Knots on the Surface of a Doughnut

The secret to understanding every knot ever tied—from your shoelaces to DNA strands—lives on the surface of a torus, the mathematical name for a doughnut shape.

In 1867, the physicist William Thomson—later Lord Kelvin—proposed that atoms were knots in the ether, the invisible medium Victorians believed filled all space. Different elements, he thought, were simply different kinds of knots. The theory was completely wrong about atoms, but it sparked a mathematical obsession that continues today. To classify knots, mathematicians needed a way to tell them apart. They found it in an unlikely place: the surface of a doughnut.

Knot theory is now one of the most active branches of topology, the study of shapes that can be stretched and bent but not torn. It has applications from molecular biology to quantum computing. And at its heart sits the torus—a shape that reveals what makes one knot fundamentally different from another.
