Why Mathematicians Proved Some Problems Can Never Be Solved Perfectly

In 1972, a 21-year-old graduate student proved that thousands of everyday problems—from scheduling airline crews to folding proteins—are mathematically doomed to remain unsolvable in any reasonable amount of time.

You're a traveling salesperson with 50 cities to visit. You want the shortest route that hits each city exactly once and returns home. Sounds simple. But to check every possible route would take longer than the age of the universe, even on the fastest supercomputer ever built. There are more possible routes through 50 cities than atoms in the observable universe.

This isn't a failure of computing power. It's a fundamental property of mathematics itself. Some problems belong to a class where finding the perfect answer requires, essentially, checking every possibility. And for these problems, there may never be a shortcut.
