Ancient Mold Turns Simple Rice Into Japan's Most Prized Foods

A fuzzy fungus cultivated for over a thousand years is the secret architect behind sake, soy sauce, and miso—transforming bland grains into flavor explosions.

Aspergillus oryzae, affectionately called koji, looks unassuming—just fluffy white or greenish fuzz growing on steamed rice. But this microscopic powerhouse is Japan's national fungus, officially designated in 2006. When koji colonizes rice, it releases enzymes that break down starches into sugars and proteins into amino acids, particularly glutamate—the source of that deeply savory umami taste. This enzymatic magic transforms simple ingredients into sake, soy sauce, miso, mirin, and rice vinegar. Japanese craftspeople have been nurturing koji for over a millennium, carefully controlling temperature and humidity to coax the mold into producing specific flavors. Without this humble fungus, Japanese cuisine as we know it simply wouldn't exist.
