Why the Law Deliberately Forgets Crimes After Enough Time Passes

In 1994, a Los Angeles man confessed to a brutal 1969 murder on national television—and prosecutors couldn't touch him because the clock had run out.

The interview aired on March 17, 1994, during a CBS special called "Confessions of Crime." James Eli Coots looked directly into the camera and admitted he had shot and killed a man named Gerald Thomas in El Monte, California, twenty-five years earlier. The confession was unambiguous. Coots described pulling the trigger during a robbery gone wrong. Millions watched. The Los Angeles County District Attorney's office took notice. And then, nothing. California's statute of limitations for murder at the time of the killing was three years. Coots walked free, protected not by evidence or alibis but by the calendar itself.

This is the strange reality of statutes of limitations: legal mechanisms that don't dispute guilt but simply declare that enough time has passed for society to stop caring. They exist in some form in virtually every legal system on Earth, covering everything from parking tickets to financial fraud. And their philosophical foundations reveal uncomfortable truths about what justice actually means.
