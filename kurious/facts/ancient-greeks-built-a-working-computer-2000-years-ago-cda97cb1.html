Ancient Greeks Built a Working Computer 2000 Years Ago

Discovered in a shipwreck, this corroded bronze device contained more sophisticated gearing than anything seen for the next 1,400 years.

In 1901, sponge divers exploring an ancient Roman shipwreck near the Greek island of Antikythera pulled up something extraordinary: a corroded lump of bronze and wood. When archaeologists examined it, they found intricate gears—at least 30 of them—meshed together with remarkable precision. This was no simple tool. The Antikythera Mechanism, dating to around 100 BCE, could predict astronomical positions, track the cycles of the Olympic Games, and forecast eclipses decades in advance. It's essentially an analog computer, built over two millennia before the digital age. Nothing this complex would appear again in the historical record until medieval European clockmakers emerged around 1300 CE.
