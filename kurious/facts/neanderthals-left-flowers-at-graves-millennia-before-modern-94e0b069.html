Neanderthals Left Flowers at Graves Millennia Before Modern Humans

Pollen from ancient flowers found in a 60,000-year-old Neanderthal grave suggests our extinct cousins mourned their dead with deliberate ritual.

In 1960, archaeologists excavating Shanidar Cave in northern Iraq made a startling discovery. Among Neanderthal remains dating back 60,000 years, they found concentrated clusters of ancient pollen—evidence suggesting someone had placed flowers alongside the dead. This wasn't random chance. The pollen came from specific medicinal and colorful plants: yarrow, cornflowers, and hollyhocks. The positioning implied intentional placement, not wind-blown accident. If true, this meant Neanderthals weren't the brutish cavemen of popular imagination. They possessed something we consider uniquely human: the capacity to grieve, to ritualize death, to perhaps believe in something beyond the physical. Our extinct relatives may have loved deeply enough to honor their dead.
