The Rotten Fish Sauce That Built an Empire

The world's first mass-produced condiment was made from fermented fish guts, shipped across three continents, and never really went away.

In 79 CE, when Vesuvius buried Pompeii under twenty feet of volcanic ash, it preserved something unexpected: dozens of clay jars filled with a pungent brown liquid. This was garum, ancient Rome's most prized condiment—a fermented fish sauce so valuable that a single liter could cost more than a month's wages for a laborer. Romans poured it on everything: roasted meats, vegetables, bread, even desserts. They mixed it into medicines. They believed it could cure dysentery, dog bites, and ulcers.

What seems like a strange historical footnote is actually the origin story of a flavor that still dominates global cuisine. The fish sauces of Southeast Asia, the colatura of southern Italy, even Worcestershire sauce—all are direct descendants of the same fermentation process that Romans perfected two thousand years ago. Garum didn't disappear. It just changed addresses.
