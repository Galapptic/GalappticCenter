Three Economists Simultaneously Discovered Why Diamonds Beat Water

In the 1870s, three men working independently on three continents solved a puzzle that had baffled thinkers since Plato: why worthless water costs nothing while useless diamonds cost fortunes.

Water keeps you alive. Diamonds just sparkle. So why do diamonds cost thousands while water flows nearly free? This question tormented economists for centuries. Adam Smith himself couldn't crack it. Then, around 1871, something remarkable happened. Three economists—William Jevons in England, Carl Menger in Austria, and Léon Walras in Switzerland—independently reached the same breakthrough. Value doesn't come from total usefulness. It comes from the last unit you consume. Your first glass of water when dying of thirst? Priceless. Your hundredth glass today? Worth almost nothing. Diamonds are scarce, so each one matters. Water is abundant, so each additional drop barely registers. This insight—marginal utility—revolutionized economic thinking overnight.
