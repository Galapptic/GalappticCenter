Lobotomies Won a Nobel Prize Before Anyone Noticed the Damage

The 1949 Nobel Prize in Medicine rewarded a procedure that turned thousands of patients into emotionally vacant shells before anyone questioned the ethics.

In 1949, Portuguese neurologist António Egas Moniz received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for developing the prefrontal lobotomy. The procedure involved severing connections in the brain's frontal lobes, supposedly treating mental illness. Moniz performed his first lobotomy in 1935, and the medical community embraced it enthusiastically. Psychiatrists hailed it as revolutionary for treating schizophrenia, depression, and anxiety. Hospitals worldwide adopted the technique, performing tens of thousands of procedures. But there was a devastating catch: doctors hadn't conducted proper long-term studies. Patients often emerged calm but fundamentally changed—emotionally flat, lacking initiative, and stripped of their personalities. The Nobel Committee had rewarded a procedure before understanding its true human cost.
