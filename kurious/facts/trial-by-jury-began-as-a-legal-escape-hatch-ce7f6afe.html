Trial by Jury Began as a Legal Escape Hatch

Medieval defendants chose jury trials not for fairness, but because juries could simply refuse to convict—a power that still exists today.

Before juries became symbols of justice, they were escape routes. In medieval England, defendants facing trial by ordeal—think boiling water or hot irons—discovered that opting for a jury of neighbors offered something extraordinary: the chance to walk free regardless of evidence. Unlike ordeals where divine judgment was supposedly absolute, juries could simply refuse to convict. This wasn't a bug in the system; it became the feature. Neighbors judging neighbors meant local sympathies, personal relationships, and community standards trumped royal law. Accused of poaching the lord's deer? Your fellow villagers might just look the other way. This power to nullify unjust laws became the jury's secret weapon, transforming a medieval workaround into a cornerstone of modern justice.
