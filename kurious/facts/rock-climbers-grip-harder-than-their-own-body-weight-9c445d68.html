Rock Climbers Grip Harder Than Their Own Body Weight

Elite climbers routinely squeeze finger holds with forces exceeding 100% of their body weight—sometimes while dangling from a ledge the width of a matchstick.

When Alex Honnold free-soloed El Capitan in 2017, ascending 914 meters of sheer granite without ropes, his life depended entirely on his fingers. Not his arms, not his legs—his fingers. At certain points on the route, the only thing preventing a fatal fall was his ability to crimp holds smaller than the edge of a credit card. The physics of how climbers cling to rock reveals something remarkable about human adaptation: their grip force doesn't just match their body weight, it exceeds it.
