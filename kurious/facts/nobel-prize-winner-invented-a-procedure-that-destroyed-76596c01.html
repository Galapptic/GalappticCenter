Nobel Prize Winner Invented a Procedure That Destroyed Thousands of Minds

The 1949 Nobel Prize in Medicine went to a man whose signature procedure left patients permanently childlike, emotionless, or dead.

Between the 1940s and 1960s, doctors performed roughly 50,000 lobotomies in the United States alone. The procedure involved severing connections in the brain's prefrontal cortex, supposedly to calm violent or mentally ill patients. Walter Freeman popularized a terrifyingly simple version: insert an ice pick-like tool through the eye socket, tap it with a hammer, and scramble the brain tissue. Performed in minutes without proper surgical training, Freeman conducted lobotomies in his office, in psychiatric hospitals, even in hotel rooms. Patients emerged docile—but many also became vacant, incontinent, or died. Despite this, lobotomies were celebrated as revolutionary, earning Freeman national fame and his colleague Egas Moniz the Nobel Prize.
