Rocks in Chile's Atacama Desert Simply Refuse to Rust

So little moisture exists in parts of the Atacama that iron-bearing rocks remain pristine for millions of years, frozen in geological time.

Stretching along Chile's northern coast, the Atacama Desert is so extraordinarily dry that some weather stations have never recorded a single drop of rain. This hyperaridity creates something remarkable: rocks simply don't rust or decompose. Normally, iron in rocks reacts with oxygen and water to form rust, while minerals break down through weathering. But without moisture, these chemical reactions grind to a halt. Boulders that would crumble elsewhere sit unchanged for millions of years. The landscape looks almost Martian—barren, ancient, and eerily preserved. Scientists have found surface deposits here that date back 23 million years, completely unweathered. It's as if someone pressed pause on geology itself.
