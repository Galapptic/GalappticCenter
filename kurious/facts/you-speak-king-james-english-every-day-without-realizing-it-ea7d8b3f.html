You Speak King James English Every Day Without Realizing It

A 1611 translation made for a king who never read it gave us phrases so embedded in English that we've forgotten they were invented.

When you say someone is "the salt of the earth," or warn that "the blind lead the blind," or insist there's "nothing new under the sun," you're quoting a book translated over four hundred years ago for a Scottish king who wanted to unite his fractious religious subjects. The King James Bible, published in 1611, did far more than standardize Protestant scripture. It crystallized the English language at a pivotal moment, embedding itself so deeply into everyday speech that most native speakers quote it weekly without the faintest idea they're doing so.
