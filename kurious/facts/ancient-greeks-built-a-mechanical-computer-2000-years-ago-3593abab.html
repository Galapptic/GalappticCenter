Ancient Greeks Built a Mechanical Computer 2000 Years Ago

Recovered from a shipwreck in 1901, this shoebox-sized device contains at least 30 precision bronze gears that could predict eclipses, track the moon's orbit, and calculate Olympic game dates.

In 1901, sponge divers exploring an ancient shipwreck near the Greek island of Antikythera hauled up a corroded lump of bronze. For decades, nobody understood what it was. Then researchers realized they were looking at the world's first known analog computer, built around 100 BCE. The Antikythera Mechanism used a sophisticated system of interlocking gears to model the movements of the sun, moon, and planets. Users could turn a hand crank and watch the cosmos unfold, predicting solar eclipses, lunar phases, and even the timing of the ancient Olympic Games. Nothing this complex would appear again for over a thousand years.
