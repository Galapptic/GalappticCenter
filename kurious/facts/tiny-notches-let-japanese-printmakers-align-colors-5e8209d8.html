Tiny Notches Let Japanese Printmakers Align Colors Perfectly Every Time

Centuries before laser-guided machinery existed, Japanese artisans achieved color registration so precise that modern printers still struggle to match it using nothing but carved wooden marks.

Japanese woodblock printing required pressing paper against multiple carved blocks—sometimes ten or more—each adding a different color. One slip meant ruined artwork. The solution was brilliantly simple: the kento. Artisans carved a right-angle notch in one corner and a straight line mark on the opposite edge of every single block. These registration marks sat outside the image area, creating fixed reference points. Printers positioned dampened paper against these guides with practiced precision, ensuring each color layer landed exactly where intended. The paper's consistent placement meant that red lips aligned with black outlines, blue waves merged seamlessly with white foam. This mechanical solution to alignment emerged during the Edo period and remains essentially unchanged today.
