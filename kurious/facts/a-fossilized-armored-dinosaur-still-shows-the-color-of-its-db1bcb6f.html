A Fossilized Armored Dinosaur Still Shows the Color of Its Skin

A 110-million-year-old armored dinosaur was preserved so completely that chemists could detect its reddish-brown pigment, and the pattern of that color reveals which predators it feared.

On March 21, 2011, heavy-equipment operator Shawn Funk was digging through oil sands at the Suncor Millennium Mine, north of Fort McMurray in Alberta, when his excavator hit something harder than the surrounding rock. It turned out to be the front half of an armored dinosaur. The animal had not been reduced to scattered bones. It lay on its back with its scales, bony plates, and spikes still in place, as if it had been laid down to rest.

Paleontologists named it Borealopelta markmitchelli, meaning "northern shield." Its species name honors Mark Mitchell, the technician at the Royal Tyrrell Museum who spent more than 7,000 hours over five and a half years freeing it from the stone. When the full description appeared in the journal Current Biology in 2017, the most striking result was not the dinosaur's shape. It was the evidence of its color.
