The Ocean's Twilight Zone Holds More Fish Than Everywhere Else

Between 200 and 1,000 meters deep, in permanent near-darkness, swims a biomass so vast it dwarfs all the fish in sunlit waters combined—and we only discovered this in the last decade.

For most of history, marine biologists assumed the deep ocean was a sparse place. Sunlight fades entirely by 1,000 meters down, so photosynthesis stops. No plants, little food, crushing pressure. How could much live there? Then came better sonar, deep-towed nets, and submersibles with cameras. The numbers stunned researchers: the mesopelagic zone—the twilight band between 200 and 1,000 meters—contains somewhere between 10 and 100 times more fish biomass than previous estimates suggested.
