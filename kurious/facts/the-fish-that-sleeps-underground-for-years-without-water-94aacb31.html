The Fish That Sleeps Underground for Years Without Water

When rivers vanish in African droughts, lungfish wrap themselves in mucus sleeping bags and wait—sometimes for four years—until the rain returns.

In 1853, a British museum received a curious shipment from West Africa: a block of dried clay. Workers broke it open expecting fossils. Instead, they found a living fish coiled inside, covered in a papery cocoon of hardened slime. When they placed it in water, the creature stirred, unfolded its eel-like body, and began to breathe. It had survived the journey in suspended animation, wrapped in its own dried mucus.

This was Europe's introduction to the African lungfish, one of the strangest survivors in the vertebrate world. While other fish die within minutes of leaving water, lungfish can endure years of drought buried in mud, their metabolism slowed to nearly nothing. They accomplish this through a biological trick so ancient it predates the dinosaurs—and so effective that scientists are now studying it to help preserve human organs for transplant.
